# Art

All sprites are original work, authored as indexed pixel data in
`content/art/sprites.toml`. There are no image files in the repository, and no
third-party art of any kind.

## Seeing the art

```bash
./build/td_app --sprites      # every sprite, labelled by id and size
./build/td_app --artcompare   # old vs new, on the real terrain, at 1x/2x/3x
./build/td_app                # in context, which is the only judgement that counts
```

Screenshots also live in `docs/previews/`.

## Asking for a change

Every sprite has an id shown on the sheet. Name it and say what is wrong:

> "`wolf_0` doesn't read as a wolf — make the head bigger and add a snout"
> "`crown_sniper` is too subtle at normal zoom"
> "the whole palette is too cold"

Sizes are cheap to change too — a sprite's `size` is declared next to it.

## The format

```toml
[palette]
"#" = "14101cff"   # single character -> RRGGBBAA

[[sprite]]
id = "slime_0"
size = [24, 24]
rows = [ "........", ... ]   # one character per pixel, '.' is transparent
```

Row widths are validated at load, so a mis-counted row fails loudly instead of
rendering as garbage. `tests/content/test_sprites.cpp` additionally asserts that
no sprite is blank, none is a solid rectangle with no silhouette, and that
animation frames agree on their dimensions.

## Where the look comes from

Three layers, and the scene needs all of them:

**Sprites** — `content/art/sprites.toml`, generated by `tools/make_sprites.py`.

**Terrain** — generated in `src/render/TileGen.cpp`. Grass and dirt are
synthesised with per-tile variation and mirrored per coordinate to break the
repeat. The road is **autotiled**: three transition pieces (a straight fringe,
an outer corner, an inner corner) authored for one orientation and rotated into
place cover every case, instead of authoring the full 47-tile blob set. A path
drawn as bare squares reads as a grid; grass lipping over its edge with proper
corners is what makes it a road.

**Atmosphere** — `Renderer::drawAtmosphere`. A vignette plus a cool-to-warm
vertical wash matching the sprites' light direction, and layered contact shadows
under every sprite. Flat ambient light everywhere is what makes a top-down scene
look like a spreadsheet of tiles.

## The palette is measured, not invented

This mattered more than every drawing technique combined. Colours were sampled
from a CC0 reference tileset and the old palette was catastrophically dark:

| | reference | what this used to be |
|---|---|---|
| grass | `#7bad2c` bright yellow-green | `#2f4a28` near-olive |
| stone | `#cccccc` near-white | `#454464` dark blue-purple |
| outline | `#404040` dark grey | `#17131f` near-black |

A dark, desaturated board makes every sprite on it look dirty no matter how well
that sprite is drawn. Towers in particular were invisible: dark blue-grey stone
against dark green grass has almost no value contrast. Light stone against bright
grass reads instantly.

Pure black outlines are also a mistake in a bright scene -- they make it look
grimy. Dark grey is enough to separate a sprite from its background.

Sample any reference before trusting your own eye:

```python
from PIL import Image; import collections
im = Image.open("reference.png").convert("RGBA")
print(collections.Counter(p[:3] for p in im.getdata() if p[3] > 200).most_common(20))
```

## The rules this set follows

These are what separate readable pixel art from mush, and were exactly what the
first draft got wrong:

1. **Everything is outlined** in near-black. Without it a sprite dissolves into
   the grass.
2. **Every material has a full ramp**, shadow through highlight — not two similar
   mid-tones. Ramps are grouped in the palette by material.
3. **One light source**, upper-left, for every sprite.
4. **Scenery is deliberately duller than actors** and is additionally dimmed when
   drawn. Bushes once looked like slimes, which made the board unreadable.
5. **Silhouette carries the meaning.** Each tower specialisation has a different
   outline — a single tall bow, twin banners, a rack of three arrows — so the
   board reads without colour.
6. **Front-facing, big head.** Measured from CC0 reference sprites: readable
   creatures at this size face the viewer and give roughly half their height to
   the head, with the silhouette cues (ears, wings) on the top edge. A side-on
   sprite only reads while it happens to be walking sideways.
7. **Three tones, not five.** A reference sprite was measured at five colours
   INCLUDING its background. Five-step ramps muddy a 24px sprite; `tri()` builds
   the three-tone ramps the creatures use.

## The tower line

Kingdom Rush's archer towers are *"manned with two archers"* -- the shooters are
visible, which is what stops a tower reading as an empty keep. So `tower_base` is
only the STRUCTURE and each specialisation is a manned platform:

| sprite | reads as |
|---|---|
| `crown_plain` | two ordinary archers, so an unspecialised tower never looks unfinished |
| `crown_sniper` | one archer, an enormous longbow, raised on a firing step |
| `crown_elf` | a leafy canopy over a timber platform -- organic, not masonry |
| `crown_hunter` | three archers shoulder to shoulder on a wide deck, quivers on the rail |

The element grows **on** the tower rather than sitting beside it as a bead:
`enchant_poison` (vines and drips), `enchant_rock` (crystals jutting out),
`enchant_quake` (ground cracks and glowing runes).

Gold levels show as **bronze / silver / gold trim** on the plinth, the convention
tower defence games use so tier reads without opening a panel.

Two things the shaft needed that were not obvious:

- **Masonry courses.** Without horizontal courses and staggered vertical joints,
  a stone shaft is just a light rectangle.
- **A wide value spread.** The stone ramp started at three tones that sat so
  close together the whole tower read as one flat column. Loopholes in
  particular have to be genuinely dark or they disappear at 1x -- and they are
  the clearest cue that the building is full of archers.

## Animation

Six frames per creature. Four is the minimum that reads as motion, six is
smooth, eight is only worth it at larger sizes. Frame count is read from the
art, so adding frames is a content change.

- **Squash and stretch preserves volume** — two pixels lost in height are added
  across the width, or the creature appears to shrink rather than compress.
- **Secondary motion** — the wolf's ears lag its head by a frame, the goblin's
  shield swings behind his body. This is what stops a cycle looking mechanical.
- **Differential rates** — the bee's wings beat twice per body hover.
- **Ground units animate off distance travelled, fliers off time**, so a
  petrified wolf stops mid-stride while a bee's wings never stop.
- Towers **recoil when they fire**, derived from the cooldown that already
  exists rather than from new animation state.

Tests enforce that every creature has at least four frames, that frames share
dimensions, and that consecutive frames actually differ — a cycle of identical
frames animates nothing.

## References

Proportions were measured from **CC0** sprites (OpenGameArt's Tiny Creatures and
others), studied and then drawn from scratch. **No reference art is in this
repository** and none ships with the game; it was downloaded to a scratch
directory, measured, and discarded.

## Terrain

Ground is generated, not authored: `src/render/TileGen.cpp` synthesises grass and
dirt with per-tile variation, and tiles are mirrored per coordinate to break the
repeat. Scenery is scattered in world space rather than baked into tiles, because
tile-baked detail always repeats with the tile and forms a visible lattice.

## Known weak spots

Honest list, in the order I would fix them:

- **No death animation.** Enemies pop out of existence into a particle burst.
- **No directional sprites.** A creature walking left looks identical to one
  walking right, because every sprite faces the camera.
- **No tower firing frames.** There is recoil, but the bow never draws and
  releases.
- **One biome.** Grass and dirt only; no water, rock, or elevation to vary a map.

## Importing sheets from an asset pack

Two things cost a full debugging pass each; both are now handled in
`tools/import_tinyswords.py`.

**Never assume a frame size.** Most Tiny Swords troop sheets are 192px, but
`Barrel_Red.png` is 6x6 of **128px**. Slicing it at 192 cropped a 2x2 block of
four goblins into each "frame", which on screen looked like a small dark
fragment rather than a creature. `detect_pitch()` measures the gap between
columns of art and snaps to a divisor of both sheet dimensions.

**Register a cycle against one shared box.** Trimming each frame to its own
bounding box re-anchors the figure every frame, so the sprite jitters and
changes size as it walks. `cycle_frames()` crops every frame of a row to the
union of their boxes.

Row 0 is Idle and row 1 is Run in this pack. Walkers want the run cycle, except
the torch goblin, whose run drags a wide flame streak that reads as noise at
tile size.

## Baked shadows

Every creature, building and prop frame in the pack already contains a soft
contact-shadow ellipse — roughly the last 6 rows of a creature sprite, with the
feet at its top edge. Two consequences:

- Drawing our own ellipse under them gives a doubled shadow. Only fliers get a
  drawn shadow, because their baked one lifts with the hover.
- The anchor point is the shadow's centre, not the art's bottom edge
  (`kEnemyShadowInset` in `Renderer.cpp`). Anchoring the art's bottom to the
  path centre sinks the figure, which is what made enemies look buried.

Scenery is foot-anchored for the same reason, and kept to ground-level props:
the pack's signpost and scarecrow are tall enough to compete with towers for
the eye, which is the one thing scenery must not do.

## Nine-slice UI

The interface draws the pack's UI kit through `SpriteAtlas::drawNine`, over the
pure geometry in `src/core/NinePatch.h` (raylib-free, so it is unit-tested).

Three things to know:

**The slice inset is 64 at source, 32 halved.** The pack's `9Slides` / `3Slides`
naming is literal — each source is a 3x3 (or 3x1) grid of 64px cells. Overlay a
64px grid on one and the lines land exactly on the border art.

**Stretchable regions TILE; only corners draw 1:1.** Scaling a 32px patterned
cell across a 1300px panel turns the parchment hatching into giant blocks. This
was the first thing that went wrong and it is obvious once seen.

**Ribbons must be drawn at their source height.** A ribbon's source is only as
tall as its inset, so its middle row has zero source height, and drawing one
taller leaves an unpainted band across the middle. `paint::ribbon` takes the
height from the texture and ignores the caller for this reason. It also keeps the
art at 1:1 — 32px stretched to 44px is a fractional scale and shimmers.

Nine-slice sources are imported **untrimmed**. Cropping transparent margins would
shift the slice lines and the corners would stop matching.

## Ink, not light text

The imported panels are light parchment and the buttons pale blue, so UI text
uses the dark ink palette in `src/ui/Paint.h`, not the HUD's light-on-dark
colours. Every painted helper also falls back to the flat prototype look when
the art is absent: `assets/sprites` is gitignored, so a fresh checkout has no UI
art and must still be playable.

`drawFitted` grows a sprite only by an **integer** factor, and only when asked
(`grow=true`). A 12px element icon in a 43px skill-tree node otherwise stays
12px, and a fractional upscale of pixel art shimmers.

## The atmosphere shader's light pools must saturate

`assets/shaders/atmosphere.fs` gives every tower a warm light pool. The first
version accumulated them with a plain sum and then applied `rgb += rgb *
gathered`. One tower was fine. Seven adjacent towers summed to roughly 7x and
blew the whole cluster out to flat white-yellow — towers, ground and props with
it.

Two changes fix it, and both matter:

- **Saturate, never sum.** `gathered = gathered / (1 + gathered)` rolls off
  smoothly toward 1 no matter how many pools overlap, so a dense cluster reads as
  bright rather than clipped.
- **A gain below 1.** The ambient multiplier is 0.94, i.e. the field is barely
  darkened, so there is nothing for the pools to "reveal" — they are decoration,
  and anything near full strength only clips. `kLightGain` is 0.34, overridable
  with `TD_FX_LIGHTGAIN`.

Pool radius was also cut from `132 + 20/level` to `96 + 16/level`: at the old
size every tower in a block sat entirely inside all of its neighbours' pools.

Reproduce with `--cluster N`, which places a dense block of maxed towers. Any
future change to the light maths should be checked against a cluster, not a
single tower — a single tower cannot show this class of bug.
